Overview

Hawk Pride Offroad is building a standard trail rating, navigation and signage system. The goal is a park that is easier to navigate, safer to explore and more intuitive for first-time visitors, without making the experience overly structured or guided.

The work starts from the existing CartoTracks map and its trail ratings. Staff and customers find that map hard to read, and most visitors rely on the GPS app to know where they are. The new system has four parts:

  1. A four-level difficulty rating that replaces the six CartoTracks classes.
  2. A primary navigation network of routes that connect the important areas of the park.
  3. Three standard sign types, each with its own job.
  4. One visual language shared by signs, markers, maps and digital navigation.

Together they let a rider recognize difficulty at a glance, know where they are, and make their own choices about where to explore.

Numbered trail symbols

The trail number goes inside its difficulty symbol. That one graphic, the trail's identity and difficulty together, is the standard trail ID on trail markers, board signs and maps.

  • White trail number, centered in the green circle, blue square or red diamond.
  • Extreme is the exception: the double diamond is too narrow for a readable number, so the number sits beside it.
  • Works with three-digit numbers and letter suffixes such as 23a.
  • Numbers must stay readable at the sign's size and viewing distance.
  • Number and symbol aren't shown as separate elements, except for Extreme or where readability requires it.

Part 1 · Difficulty ratings

Rating principles

  • Terrain defines difficulty, not equipment. Ratings explain what to expect on the trail and give general vehicle guidance. They don't require specific tire sizes, lockers or modifications.
  • The hardest unavoidable section sets the rating, under normal conditions. A difficult obstacle with an easy bypass can be marked separately from the easier route.
  • Equipment guidance belongs on reference material (the park legend, map and website), not on every sign.
  • Vehicle restrictions are separate from difficulty. Limits such as ATV-only are posted only where they apply.
  • No special per-trail colors. The discontinued blue Polaris Outfitter Trail color is retired.

Part 2

The primary navigation network

The organizing foundation of the system. The park has many interconnected numbered trails with no obvious hierarchy. A smaller set of existing trails is chosen as primary navigation routes to help riders cross the park, reach major destinations and build a mental map of how it connects.

Primary routes

  • Connect major areas and recognizable landmarks.
  • Are identified with street-style trail signs.
  • Appear prominently on the map.
  • Give a framework for orientation without telling people where to ride.

Everything else

  • Not every trail is part of the network.
  • Other trails stay open for exploration and are identified with trail markers.
  • A trail's role in the network and its difficulty are independent: a primary route can have any rating, but essential destinations should have practical, accessible connections.

Part 3

The three sign types

Each sign type shows difficulty with the same colors, in a way that fits its job.

Street signsBoard signsTrail markersReading a board sign

Type 1 · Street signs

Purpose: Identify the key trails that make up the primary navigation network.

Design requirements

  • Reflective metal, standard street-sign format and mounting hardware.
  • Large, high-contrast lettering.
  • Trail name and, where practical, the trail number.
  • The whole background is the trail's difficulty color.
  • A navigation route badge where the trail is part of a route.
  • No difficulty symbol needed. The background already says it: a purple street sign is Extreme, with or without the double diamond.

When to use

Only on the main navigation network: at the start of each main navigation trail and wherever two main navigation trails meet. Like road-name signs.

Hawk Pride standard: 6″ tall; 24″ wide, or 30″ for long names. Green Line plaque 3″ tall, mounted below.

Type 2 · Board signs

Purpose: Help riders make navigation decisions at major intersections.

Design requirements

  • Larger printed PVC signs listing trails or destinations, each with a directional arrow.
  • Each trail line has the trail name and its numbered trail symbol (e.g. a white number in a green circle for Easy).
  • Arrow color carries the meaning: green = Easy, blue = Moderate, red = Difficult, purple = Extreme, black = destination or general wayfinding.
  • Can point to individual trails or to places such as the campground, check-in or important trail destinations.

When to use

At major decision points, where riders need more guidance than a street sign gives.

Hawk Pride standard: 24″ wide, every entry a fixed 4″ band: 24×6 (1 entry, no header), 24×12, 24×16, 24×20 and 24×24″ for 2–5 entries. See the design guide.

Type 3 · Trail markers

Purpose: Identify individual trails and help riders confirm they are on the intended route.

Design requirements

  • Small, inexpensive posts: the smallest and most numerous part of the system.
  • The numbered trail symbol.
  • A navigation route badge where that segment is part of a route.
  • A directional arrow when the trail's path needs clarifying.
  • Durable, weather-resistant, easily replaced decals.

When to use

On the trail wherever riders need identification, reassurance or direction. For example, along an Easy trail, green circle markers confirm you're still on it.

Part 3 · Sign types

Reading a board sign

  1. Start with the symbol. Its shape and color give the difficulty; the number inside identifies the trail.
  2. Read the name. Names match the park map and the app.
  3. Look for the badge. GREEN LINE means that trail is on the Green Line route.
  4. Follow the arrow. It's colored by difficulty, or black for destinations. Straight-ahead entries are listed first, then left, then right.
  5. Sign ID. The code in the bottom border (here GL-20) identifies the sign if you need to report a problem.

Part 5

Navigation routes

Primary navigation routes such as the Green Line are separate from the trails that make them up. Thompson Trail (71), for example, is a Moderate (blue) trail that is part of the Green Line.

Core principle

Every trail has its own identity and difficulty. A route is a connected journey through several trails. Signs show three things independently:

  • Trail number and name: which trail this is.
  • Difficulty color and symbol: how hard the terrain is.
  • Navigation route badge: which route it belongs to.

So riders can follow the Green Line and still know the difficulty of each trail along it.

The route badge

  • Each route gets its own distinctive, named badge.
  • The Green Line badge is a green rectangle with "GREEN LINE" in white.
  • It must look clearly different from the green Easy circle. Hawk Pride uses a deeper green with square corners.
  • More routes can be added later using the same convention.

Where it appears

  • Street signs: a small Green Line badge goes with the sign; the background still shows the trail's own difficulty.
  • Board signs: beside the trail information and arrow, wherever the board directs riders along a Green Line trail.
  • Trail markers: in addition to the numbered trail symbol.

Route continuity

The Green Line is one continuous route across several trails. Where it turns or moves onto another trail, directional Green Line markers (the badge with an arrow) show which way to go to stay on it. Badges follow the actual route: if only part of a trail is on the Green Line, only that part is badged.
