Executive overview
We are developing a standardized trail rating, navigation, and signage system for Hawk Pride
Offroad.
The objective is to make the park easier to navigate, safer to explore, and more intuitive for
first-time visitors, without turning the experience into something overly structured or
guided.
We are working from the existing CartoTracks map, assuming its trail ratings are accurate.
The map is widely considered difficult to read by both staff and customers, and most
visitors rely on the GPS-enabled app to understand their location.
Our solution has four fundamental components:
1. A simplified, four-level trail difficulty rating system.
2. A primary navigation network connecting important areas of the park.
3. Three standardized sign types, each with a distinct purpose.
4. A consistent visual language connecting trail signs, markers, maps, and digital
navigation.
The system should allow a rider to recognize trail difficulty at a glance, identify where they
are, and make their own decisions about where to explore.
Part 1 - The trail difficulty rating system
We are reducing the existing six CartoTracks classifications to four difficulty levels, plus
access roads.

IMPORTANT RATING PRINCIPLES
Terrain defines difficulty, not equipment. We will explain what riders should expect on the
trail and provide general vehicle guidance, but not require specific tire sizes, lockers, or
modifications for particular ratings.
The rating should reflect the hardest unavoidable section under normal conditions. A
difficult obstacle with an easy bypass can be identified separately from the easier route.
Equipment guidance belongs primarily on the park legend, map, website, or other reference
material rather than on every sign.
Vehicle restrictions, such as ATV-only access, are separate from difficulty. They should be
communicated only where applicable.
We will not retain special classification colors for individual trails, such as the discontinued
Blue Polaris Outfitter Trail.
Part 2 — The primary navigation network
This is the organizational foundation of the system.
=== PAGE 6 ===
Today, the park contains a large number of interconnected numbered trails without a
particularly obvious hierarchy.
We intend to identify a smaller subset of existing trails that will serve as primary navigation
routes.
These routes will help riders travel through the park, reach major destinations, and establish
a mental picture of how the park connects.
HOW THE NETWORK WILL FUNCTION
Primary navigation routes will:
● Connect major areas and recognizable landmarks.
● Be identifiable through street-style trail signs.
● Appear prominently on the map.
● Provide a useful orientation framework without prescribing where people should ride.
Other trails remain available for exploration and are identified using trail markers.
Not every trail will be part of the primary navigation system.
Importantly, a trail's role as a primary navigation route and its difficulty classification are
independent. A primary route can technically have any difficulty rating, although we should
ensure practical, accessible connections between essential visitor destinations.
WHAT REMAINS UNDECIDED
We have not yet selected the specific trails that will form the primary navigation network.
This must be determined before we finalize sign locations.
Part 3 — The three sign types
We have established three general navigation sign types. Each communicates difficulty
using the same colors, but differently according to its purpose.
TYPE 1 — STREET SIGNS
Purpose: Identify the key trails that make up the
primary navigation network.
=== PAGE 7 ===
Standard reflective metal street signs on posts. The entire background color indicates trail
difficulty.
Every intersection between primary navigation routes should have the necessary street
signs. These are similar to conventional road-name signs.
DESIGN REQUIREMENTS:
● Reflective metal construction.
● Standard street-sign format and mounting hardware.
● Large, high-contrast lettering.
● Trail name and potentially trail number.
● Full sign background colored according to trail difficulty.
A purple street sign identifies an Extreme route even if the double-diamond symbol is
absent.
HOW AND WHEN TO USE:
These signs are reserved for the main navigation network, not every trail in the park. These
should only be placed at the beginning of main navigation trails and at intersection of main
navigation trails.
TYPE 2 — BOARD SIGNS
Purpose: Help riders make navigation decisions at major intersections.
Larger printed, PVC signs containing destinations or trail
names with directional arrows.
The color of each arrow communicates its meaning: trail
difficulty or general destination.
These are placed at major decision points where someone
needs more guidance than a street sign provides.
They may identify individual trails or direct visitors toward
locations such as the campground, check-in, or important
trail destinations.
=== PAGE 8 ===
DESIGN REQUIREMENTS
Each board should have the trail name, number and difficulty symbol.
● Green = Easy trail
● Blue = Moderate trail
● Red = Difficult trail
● Purple = Extreme trail
● Black = Destination or general wayfinding
HOW AND WHEN TO USE:
At trail intersections - Where to buy
TYPE 3 — TRAIL MARKERS
Purpose: Identify individual trails and help riders confirm
they are following the intended route.
Small, inexpensive posts with trail number decals and colored
difficulty symbols.
These are the smallest and most numerous elements of the
system.
They identify which trail someone is following.
For example, along an Easy trail, a rider encounters green
circle markers confirming they remain on that trail.
DESIGN REQUIREMENTS
A typical marker should contain:
● The existing trail identification number.
● The colored difficulty symbol.
● A directional indicator where needed.
The intended placement includes trail entrances, confusing intersections, immediately after
major junctions, and places where a route becomes difficult to follow.
=== PAGE 9 ===
The marker is not intended as an expensive miniature street sign. It should be a simple,
durable, easily replaceable post and decal system.
WHEN TO USE:
Installed on trail where they are needed for identification, reassurance, and navigation.

Part 5 - NAVIGATION ROUTE IDENTIFICATION
Primary navigation routes, such as the Green Line, are separate from the individual trails
that make up those routes.
=== PAGE 10 ===
For example, Thompson Trail (#71) is a Moderate (Blue) trail that forms part of the Green
Line navigation system.
To distinguish these two concepts, Hawk Pride will use a standardized Navigation Route
Badge.
The badge identifies membership in a primary navigation system without replacing or
interfering with the trail's existing name, number, or difficulty classification.
CORE PRINCIPLE
Every trail has its own identity and difficulty classification. A primary navigation route is a
connected journey through multiple trails.
The signage system must communicate both independently:
● Trail Number and Name: Identifies the individual trail.
● Difficulty Color and Symbol: Communicates the terrain difficulty.
● Navigation Route Badge: Identifies membership in a larger navigational system.
This allows visitors to follow the Green Line while understanding the difficulty of each
individual trail they encounter.
DESIGN REQUIREMENTS
● Each primary navigation system will have its own distinctive, named route badge.
● The Green Line will use a green rectangular badge displaying "GREEN LINE" in white
lettering.
● The badge must be visually distinct from the green circle used to identify Easy trails.
● Badges will be incorporated into existing street signs, board signs, and trail markers
where appropriate.
● Additional navigation systems can be introduced later using the same design
convention.
HOW THE BADGE WILL BE USED
Street Signs
On primary navigation trails that form part of the Green Line, a small Green Line badge will
accompany the standard street sign. The street sign's background color will continue to
represent the difficulty of the individual trail.
=== PAGE 11 ===
Board Signs
Where a board sign directs riders along a trail that forms part of the Green Line, the Green
Line badge will appear alongside the trail information and directional arrow.
Trail Markers
Trail markers along the Green Line will display the route badge in addition to the existing
trail number and difficulty symbol.
ROUTE CONTINUITY AND DIRECTION
The Green Line is a continuous route that follows multiple existing trails. At intersections
where the Green Line changes direction or transitions between trails, directional Green Line
markers will indicate which way riders should travel to remain on the route.
These markers will use the Green Line badge with an accompanying directional arrow.
Route identification must follow the actual Green Line path. If only a portion of an existing
trail is part of the Green Line, badges and directional indicators should apply only to that
portion.
